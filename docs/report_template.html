1. Motivation

Who has the problem. Yale has about 15,000 students spread across Yale College and more than a dozen graduate and professional schools. Most social life stays inside one's own school and cohort. A first-year MBA who loves Formula 1 or EDM festivals usually has no way to find the medical student or the Law School student who loves the same things, even though they live a few blocks apart.

How they handle it today. I asked two Yale students (Abi, Law School, and William, School of Medicine) how they found people the last time they wanted company for an event or a new activity:

  • Abi: "friends' friends or Facebook groups", signing up for events on Eventbrite and "hope to find someone there", and joining random group chats.
  • William: "just close circles, friends' friends".

What it costs them. Both said it takes a few days (William: 5 to 10 days), and Abi called it "difficult to coordinate". The real cost is often that people simply do not go: tickets to a game or a festival go unused, or they go alone. The information they need exists (who likes F1, who is free on weekend mornings, who already bought a ticket), but it is scattered across group chats, Yale Connect club pages and personal networks, and nobody can search it.

The job to be done: "I want to do X this week. Who at Yale, in any school, would enjoy doing it with me, and how do I reach them without spamming ten group chats?"

2. What is new

Students already have tools for parts of this. None of them connects what you want to do with who at Yale wants to do it too.

AlternativeWhat it does wellWhat it misses for this job
Yale Connect (club directory)Official list of clubs and their eventsLists groups, not people. No matching, no "who is going", no cross-school discovery of individuals.
GroupMe / WhatsApp group chatsFast once you are in the right chatYou must already know the chat exists. Hard to search, noisy, and contact details are shared with everyone.
Eventbrite, PartifulTickets and RSVPsShows events, but not which Yale students who share your interests are going.
Bumble BFF, Hinge, MeetupFriend or date matching with polished profilesOpen to anyone in the city, not Yale-verified; swiping on photos rather than on shared plans and interests.
ChatGPTCan suggest activities and write a nice messageKnows nothing about who at Yale is free, interested or going. It cannot introduce you to anyone.

What Bulldog Circles does differently.

  • Events and people in one place. A shared calendar of meetups tonight and big trips (F1 races, EDC, museums, day trips), and for every event you can see who from Yale is going and connect with them.
  • AI matching that explains itself. The matcher reads your profile and every other student's, then says why you should meet, using only facts both of you wrote (for example "you both listed EDM and are going to the Formula 1 watch party").
  • Across every Yale school, with an undergrad / grad filter.
  • Safety built for students: chat opens only when both say yes, no emails or phone numbers are shared, the AI flags hurtful messages before they are sent, and dating is opt-in and hidden by default.

Honest comparison. Hinge and Bumble have far better photos, scale and polish, and Yale Connect is the official source for clubs. Bulldog Circles is narrower: it only works if enough Yale students join, and the sample data is fictional until they do. Its edge is the combination: Yale-only, plan-first, with an AI that explains every match.

3. The agents

The app uses six small agents built with Pydantic AI, all on gpt-6-luna. Each has one job, a fixed instruction file in prompts/, and a typed output, so the app can check what comes back. Everything that does not need judgment (browsing, the calendar, filters, safety rules) is plain Python with no AI cost.

AgentRoleTools / output
IntakeTurns the free text "Describe yourself" into a structured profileOutput: Profile (interests, availability, what they are looking for, a clean bio)
MatcherPicks the best 3–5 people and 1–3 clubs, and writes the reason for eachTools: search_students, search_clubs. Output: MatchList
IcebreakerDrafts a friendly first message for a matchOutput: one short message (the user edits and sends)
Message checkReviews chat messages, event posts and prompt answers before they go outOutput: ok + a gentle nudge if not
Photo checkScreens profile photos (one real face, nothing inappropriate)Output: ok + reason
Selfie checkVerifies a live selfie copies a random pose and matches the profile photoOutput: ok + reason (selfie is not stored)

How the agents are organised

The organisation is a pipeline with code gates: Python decides who is even allowed to be seen, an agent does the judgment, and Python checks the agent's answer before the user sees it. Agents never call each other directly and never see private data.

Figure 2. Agent organisation. Usage limits (6 requests and 60,000 tokens per run) wrap every agent call.

Models and why

All agents use gpt-6-luna through Portkey, the only model the course key allows. It is also a good fit: the tasks are short, structured and frequent (profile extraction, ranking 12 candidates, one-line reviews), so a fast, inexpensive model with structured outputs and tool calling is the right size. A bigger model would add cost and latency without changing the result much, because Python already narrows the candidates and checks the answers.

One real run, traced step by step

This is a real run from October 26, 2026 (saved in docs/evidence/traced_run.json), for a test student who wrote:

"2nd-year MBA at SOM. Into Formula 1, hiking, jazz and trying ramen spots. Free weekday evenings
and weekend mornings. Looking for a small friend group outside SOM."
  1. Intake agent → Profile. Returned interests: ["formula 1", "hiking", "jazz", "ramen"], availability: ["weekday evenings", "weekend mornings"], looking_for: "a small friend group outside SOM". It returned name: "unknown" because no name was in the text. That is why the app now has a required Name field that overrides the AI's guess.
  2. Code gate. Python built the pool: every sample student who is not the user, not blocked, and passes the level filter (52 students). The agent only ever sees public_view() records: no emails, injected bios hidden, contact details scrubbed.
  3. Matcher agent, tool call 1: search_students(interests=["formula 1","hiking","jazz","ramen"], limit=12) → 12 candidates (first: Clara W., School of Medicine, hiking, free weekend mornings).
  4. Matcher agent, tool call 2: search_clubs(tags=["formula 1","hiking","jazz","ramen","food","music","outdoors",…]) → 8 clubs.
  5. Matcher output → code check. Five matches, each with shared interests and a reason, for example: "Clara is a Med School student who enjoys hiking and is looking for a small friend group; your weekend-morning availability overlaps for a hike together." The app checked every ID against the pool: 0 invented IDs dropped. Two clubs suggested: GPSS and the Yale Farm.
  6. Icebreaker agent. Drafted: "Hey Clara! I'm into hiking too, would you be up for a weekend-morning walk on one of the trails near campus sometime?"
  7. Logged usage: 2 requests, 7,695 input tokens (2,437 cached), 1,016 output tokens, $0.001183.

At Yale's full size (~15,000 students, if half were active) the AI bill would be around $70 a month, about the price of one club dinner. The cost driver is the matcher, because it reads 12 candidate profiles plus the club list. Hosting and the database would cost more than the AI.

What we did to cut cost, and the trade-offs

  • Prompt caching. The 28-club catalog sits in the matcher's fixed instructions, so it is cached between runs. Measured: 56% of matcher input tokens were cached (4,318 of 7,695 on average), billed at one tenth of the price. That cuts the matcher's input cost by about half.
  • Code first, AI second. Python removes ineligible people (not opted in, blocked, wrong level, not going to the chosen event) before the agent sees anyone, so the prompt stays small.
  • No AI where it isn't needed. Browsing, the calendar, interest circles, "who's going" and the "More people you might like" list (ranked by shared interests and free time) cost $0. Trade-off: those extra people come without an AI-written reason.
  • Hard limits. Every run is capped at 6 requests and 60,000 tokens, so a confused agent cannot loop or overspend.
  • Trimmed records. The agent receives a short public view of each student (no email, no dating details), which is safer and cheaper.

5. Safety

Before vs. after. Finding company for an event took both testers a few days before (William: 5–10 days) through friends of friends and group chats. In the app, it took 46 seconds to 1 minute 50 seconds. Even allowing a day for the other person to reply, that is a saving of several days of coordination per plan.

In their words. Abi: "Multi use app." William: "Easy to set up events and to find friends."

What they wanted changed, and what I did. Abi could not find how to turn on dating, and gender did not show on dating profiles. William felt the home page "doesn't look like home page" and asked for events to connect with his calendar. I also spotted in their screenshots that a message could be sent several times while the AI was checking it. All six issues were fixed the same day: a clearly marked Dating box with numbered steps, gender on cards, a personal "Hi William, here's your week" home banner, an Add to calendar button (.ics file for Apple, Google or Outlook), and a send lock with a 10-second duplicate guard.

Value at scale (estimate). If 1,000 Yale students each found company for one extra event a month, that is 1,000 plans a month that otherwise would have taken days of messaging, or would not have happened. The AI cost of that is about $9 a month.

7. Limits and next steps

Limits

  • Sample data. The 48 other students and 11 meetups are fictional, so matches are only as good as the sample. The app only becomes useful once real students join (the classic network-effect problem).
  • Small user test. Two testers on one day is evidence, not proof. William rated it 3/5, mainly about navigation, which I have since changed but not re-tested.
  • Sign-in is a demo. Any @yale.edu address can sign in without a code unless email sending is configured. A real launch needs Yale NetID (CAS) login.
  • Runs locally. SQLite on one laptop; chats refresh every 3 seconds instead of real-time push.
  • Regex safety has gaps. Pattern checks catch common injections and contact formats, not every creative variation; the AI message check is a second layer, not a guarantee.
  • Club data was assembled by hand and should be checked against Yale Connect.

Next steps

  1. Yale NetID login and hosting (a small cloud server with a real database), then a pilot with one SOM section and one residential college.
  2. Replace sample events with a feed from Yale Connect and student-posted events, with moderation.
  3. Re-test with 5–10 students, measuring how many matches lead to an actual meetup.
  4. Group matching: suggest small groups of 3–5 people for the same event, not just pairs.
  5. Calendar sync both ways (free/busy) so the matcher can suggest a time, not just a person.
